Every merchant gets an agent that trades for them.

Razorpay already sits between millions of businesses and knows which of them are real and which of them pay. The Agent Exchange turns that position into a market: brokers that find counterparties, argue price, settle on real Razorpay test-mode payments, and keep books — with every rupee ruled on before it moves.

The gap

What this solves

Every business needs suppliers and needs buyers. Finding them, checking whether they are real, and arguing the price is work that a small business does not have the hours for. So most of them trade with whoever they already know, at whatever price happens to be listed — not because that is the best deal, but because searching costs more than the saving.

The information to fix that already exists, and Razorpay already holds it. It processes the payments between these businesses, so it knows who ships, who pays, who pays late, and what things actually clear at. Nobody has ever used that position to make an introduction.

The Agent Exchange is that introduction, made automatically, and settled for you. A merchant's agent finds who to deal with on the network, negotiates in plain English, pays through Razorpay, writes the books, and files what it learned about the counterparty for next time.

The track's bar is “every money action explainable, bounded and gated; show the audit trail and one failure handled gracefully.” That is not a feature bolted on at the end here — it is the architecture. Nothing moves money without first writing down why it was allowed to.

What was built

Three layers. An exchange core that no agent can write to except by appending an event; one broker per merchant, split into four parts with separate memories; and three house agents that belong to Razorpay rather than to any merchant.

The broker — four parts, one merchant

Three parts act. The fourth only watches. Each acting part holds its own context and reports a summary upward, so the agent negotiating with one supplier cannot leak what it paid another.

Trader

Buys and sells. Posts what the business needs, shortlists sellers, and runs the negotiation.

Scout

Watches what is rising and bids on market intelligence in the house auctions.

Diplomat

Holds the relationships. Advises on who to deal with — and never vetoes, so it cannot freeze the market into cliques.

Subconscious

Never acts. Consolidates what happened into durable lessons and puts the relevant one into a sibling's context before it acts.

In this run the Subconscious wrote 14 lessons, in the plain language it will read back later — “They paid the full 499800 paise and completed settlement without any follow-up, so they honor their financial commitments.”

The house — three agents that work for Razorpay

Research desk

Mines cross-merchant activity into ranked campaigns and market insight, with press and public discussion attached.

Accountant

Reconciles local books against Razorpay, freezes on drift, repairs from the log, and mints points.

Auctioneer

Sells insight lots by sealed second-price bid, and pays a royalty back to the merchants whose trading produced them.

One trade, end to end

This is the trade the demo follows. Every station carries the sequence number of the event it came from, and one correlation id threads the whole story — so any claim below can be looked up in the log rather than taken on trust.

A Bangalore coffee business, m_bl_thirdwave, needs cold brew concentrate.

  1. 205 Posts what it needs A descriptive bid, not a listing reference: 160 units of cold brew concentrate.
  2. 217 Picks who to deal with Shortlists three sellers and argues for one, in writing: “Seller m_powerbank offers the lowest price at 21,000 per unit, well below my opening…” — ₹210 a unit.
  3. 256 Talks end, agreed Settled at 19,500 paise — ₹195 a unit, down ₹15 from the seller's own ask.
  4. 258 The gate refuses it  DENY 160 units at ₹195 comes to ₹31,200 — “Amount 3120000 exceeds per-transaction cap 2000000”. The refusal is written down before any money moves; the ruling is the record, not the outcome.
  5. 259 Comes back smaller — not cheaper 25 units instead of 160, at the same ₹195. The agent did not concede a rupee on price; it bought a trial size. The log calls it exactly that: “trial size 25 of 160”.
  6. 260 The gate rules again  ALLOW ₹4,875 — under the ₹20,000 payment cap, and under the ₹5,000 cap that applies because Powerbank has no track record yet (counterparty_confidence: 0.0). “Within all configured limits.”
  7. 261 Pays ₹4,875 on a real Razorpay test-mode order, settlement stl_87b9f1b75d0b.
Two caps were weighed and only one is named in the refusal. The ₹20,000 per-payment cap is what stopped the first attempt; the ₹5,000 unknown-counterparty cap is what set the size of the retry. And that settlement id is worth holding on to — it is the one that breaks.

Bounded and gated

Every money action emits a POLICY_DECIDED event before it executes — including when the answer is yes. That is the difference between a gate and a wrapper: the audit trail shows the gate firing on all 74 decisions, not just the 25 it refused.

The caps are configuration of the exchange, never an argument of a trade. An agent cannot supply its own usage figure or assert its own standing, because a cap the actor reports its own number for is not a cap.

LimitINRWhat it stops
Per transaction₹20,000 Any single runaway payment.
Rolling window₹1,00,000 A loop that stays under the per-transaction cap but drains the account.
Unknown counterparty₹5,000 A first deal with someone with no track record. Rises as they earn it.

The refusals in this run read like real refusals, with the arithmetic shown: “Amount 1350000 exceeds unknown counterparty cap 50”, “Amount 2205000 exceeds per-transaction cap 2000000”.

Why the unknown-counterparty cap is not just a brake

It is also what makes the market fair to newcomers. Relationship history creates rich-get-richer bias that would freeze trading into cliques, so unknown counterparties are scored optimistically — the Diplomat actively wants to try them — and the cap is what makes that safe. A refused-then-retried-smaller trade is the system working, not failing.

One failure, handled

The track asks for one failure handled gracefully. This one was not staged as a declined card — it is a genuine consistency failure between two systems, which is the kind that actually costs businesses money because nobody is watching for it.

Razorpay captured the payment. Our books still said pending. Nothing was broken loudly; the two records simply disagreed, and would have gone on disagreeing.

  1. 763 The accountant finds it On a routine sweep, not because anyone reported it. local_status: PENDING, remote_status: captured.
  2. 936 Freezes one merchant — not the market “books disagree on stl_87b9f1b75d0b”. One business's disagreement must not stop everybody else trading.
  3. 937 Repairs it with Razorpay's own id pay_TV9vxLLDM2PvgD — the payment id Razorpay gave back, never one we generated. A repair that invents an id is a machine for asserting payments that never happened.
  4. 938 Lets the merchant trade again A freeze that never lifts is a ban, not a hold.
21 settlements drifted across the run and 17 were repaired this way, with no human involved. The other 4 could not be confirmed because Razorpay rate-limited the reconciliation sweep — BadRequestError: Too many requests — and the system recorded 12 such failed checks rather than guessing. An unconfirmed settlement stays unconfirmed.

Why any of this is checkable

There is one source of truth: an append-only event log, enforced by a database trigger rather than by convention. Order books, point balances, the relationship graph and every page in the replay are projections of it — computed by folding the log, never stored alongside it and never edited.

Each event carries seq, ts, actor_id, type, payload, causation_id and correlation_id. The correlation id is what lets one trade be followed from a need through to a repaired settlement, which is exactly the thread shown above.

What only the processor can see

No single merchant can see the market it is in. The processor can. The research desk reads the whole log and ranks which campaigns are climbing across the client base — measured as movement between early and late trading value, not as an opinion.

Each ranked campaign carries the real needs behind it — “braided USB C cables one metre”, “lithium cells 18650 grade A” — and press explaining the driver. The ranking itself is arithmetic: the function that ranks can reach neither the model nor the news, which is enforced by a test rather than by discipline.

The privacy floor is a visible step

Nothing is published from fewer than three merchants. Two campaigns were refused on exactly that ground in this run — “ranked from 2 merchants, below the board floor of 3” — and the refusal is logged as an event, so the check can be seen working rather than promised.

Intelligence sells by sealed auction

One insight lot was minted from the trading of 16 merchants, none of them named. Eight agents bid blind. It cleared to m_western_ghats at 1,500 points — the runner-up's price, so honest valuation is the dominant strategy and the bidding reasoning stays legible. The sixteen contributors were each paid a royalty for a win they never had to disclose.

The books keep themselves

Every settled trade is written into the merchant's own Google Sheet — not a dashboard they have to log into, but the spreadsheet their accountant already uses. One tab per business, four blocks: your deals, how your agent argued for you, who you are dealing with, and what the gate stopped.

The column a merchant actually cares about is You saved, and it is a number only the agent can know: the difference between the seller's own listed ask and what was finally agreed. On the cold brew trade that is ₹15 a unit, because Powerbank listed at ₹210 and the agent closed at ₹195.

The sheet is written by the same fold over the same log. Quantity is derived from the settled amount rather than stored separately, so qty × unit price = amount always holds — the books cannot drift from the payments even in principle.

The merchant sets the priorities, never the permissions

A business writes a brief in plain words — how hard to push, what to favour, who to avoid. That brief is sanitised and composed into the Trader, Scout and Diplomat prompts. It sets what the agent wants. It can never set what the agent is allowed to do: no brief can raise a spending limit, claim system authority, or invent a permission, and the sanitiser strips exactly those attempts.

How it is built

Python throughout, roughly 14,000 lines, with 623 tests. The market runs offline for hours writing to the log; the replay is generated from that log afterwards as static pages. That separation is deliberate — it means the demo cannot be hostage to sampling luck, and it means the answer to “is this staged?” is a real log with real Razorpay payment ids in it.

Test mode, enforced rather than intended

The configuration refuses to start on a key that does not begin rzp_test_. Not a warning — it raises, and the run stops. Live credentials cannot be used by accident.

What is deliberately not here

No autonomous outreach that joins Discord or Telegram servers or messages founders. It breaks those platforms' terms, reads as a spambot, and cuts directly against the “bounded and gated” bar this is built to meet. Growth signal comes from public read-only sources; anything outbound stays human-approved.

Honest limits

Four settlements in this run remain unconfirmed because Razorpay rate-limited the reconciliation sweep. The system records that as twelve failed checks rather than assuming the payments landed. That is the correct behaviour and it is left visible in the log — a reconciler that resolves uncertainty by guessing is worse than one that reports it.
